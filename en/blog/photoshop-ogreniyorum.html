Learning Photoshop: what stuck with me from my first assignments

The power of the History Brush, the product feel a reflection gives, and Layer Comps keeping three designs in one file. What I learned from three Motion Graphics assignments.

This term I started learning Photoshop from scratch in the Motion Graphics I course. Before, I only cropped photos and adjusted brightness. Three assignments later, I see Photoshop less as a photo app and more as a tool that teaches you to think in layers. Here is what surprised me most in each assignment.

1. History Brush: painting time backwards

In the first assignment I turned an entire image black and white. Then I picked the History Brush and painted only over the foreground character. Wherever the brush touched, the image went back to its colour state; the background stayed grey. Now the eye goes straight to the character.

What struck me most: the History Brush doesn't erase, it paints from the past. Whichever step you choose as the source in the History panel, the brush brings that moment back. Everything you did is stored somewhere, and you can call back as much of it as you want, wherever you want.

The easiest way to say what matters in an image is to take the colour away from everything else.

2. Product ad: reflection and shadow

In the second assignment I made an ad visual for a phone. I cut the product out of its background and put it on a light lilac backdrop. Two small things made the real difference: a reflection and a shadow. Without them the phone looked like it was floating; with them it became a real product standing on a glossy table.

  1. 1Duplicated the product layer and flipped it vertically.
  2. 2Aligned the copy right under the product.
  3. 3Drew a top-to-bottom gradient on a layer mask so the reflection fades away.
  4. 4Lowered the opacity and added a soft shadow underneath.
  5. 5Wrote three short slogans next to it: few words, lots of space.

The lesson here was masks. With a mask you never really delete a pixel, you just hide it. If you don't like it, you fix the mask and the image stays intact.

3. Layer Comps: three designs in one file

In the sixth assignment I took the real Demre Municipality website apart and rebuilt it in Photoshop. First I set up a 12-column grid with the 960 Grid System; the logo, menu, news, gallery and footer snapped to those columns. Then I made every section its own layer.

The real magic was Layer Comps. You save which layers are visible and where they sit, like a snapshot. I saved three layouts: news on top in one, the gallery in another, the big photo lower in the third. Same file, one click to switch, no need to keep three separate files.

Five lessons from three assignments

  • Think in layers: when everything is separate, changes are easy.
  • Hide, don't delete: masks and the History Brush keep work reversible.
  • Name your layers: "Banner Image" instead of "Layer 37" saves a lot of time later.
  • Grid first: if you place things before setting up 12 columns, everything drifts.
  • Small details, big difference: a reflection and a shadow turn an "assignment" into an "ad".

The course has three more projects; I'll add them here and on the Projects page as I finish them. I'm still very new to Photoshop, but now I can't look at an image without wondering how many layers it's made of.
